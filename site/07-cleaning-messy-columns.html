Chapter 7 · Cleaning Messy Columns

Real spreadsheets have real mess. Not hero-movie mess — small, boring, human mess: extra spaces, surprise capitalization, one column holding three facts, the same customer existing twice as two people. This chapter is a calm toolkit for exactly that mess, and your AI is a natural at it.

The specimen

Open examples/messy_names.csv. Seven rows in one column (“MessyName”), typed by seven different people who were each in a hurry:

"  maya   chen "
"LUIS ORTEGA"
"priya patel "
"June  Tanaka"
"    Omar Haddad"
"omar   haddad "
"GRACE   okafor  "

Two things jump out if you look with spreadsheet eyes:

  1. Beauty faults — leading spaces, trailing spaces, rages of capital — all styles, all fixable in one pass.
  2. One real problem: rows 5 and 6 are the same person, Omar Haddad, typed two different ways. Cleaning isn’t cosmetic — it’s what lets COUNTIF, SUMIF, and deduping see them as one.

The three cleaning formulas that handle 90% of cases

Freed by the sheet-map prompt pattern from Chapter 1:

My sheet has one column: headers in row 1, data in A2:A8 — messy names
like "  maya   chen ". Give me formulas to put in B2 (filled down)
that produce clean names: no extra leading/trailing spaces, no doubled
inner spaces, and consistent capitalization. Explain each in one line.

You’ll get some blend of the classic trio:

Formula What it does by hand
=TRIM(A2) Trims leading and trailing spaces, and squashes runs of inner spaces to one
=PROPER(A2) Capitalizes each word: maya chen → Maya Chen
=SUBSTITUTE(A2," "," ") A surgical replace — rarely needed by hand since TRIM already collapses inner spaces

Combining them yourself would look like =PROPER(TRIM(A2)) (inner-after-outer is fine, and your AI will write it for you anyway). The honest beginner move is simple: type =PROPER(TRIM(A2)) in B2, fill down, and look at the column gently.

That produces our clean list for the seven rows:

Maya Chen
Luis Ortega
Priya Patel
June Tanaka
Omar Haddad
Omar Haddad      ← same person, now seen as the same person
Grace Okafor

And COUNTIF is now equipped to tell the truth:

=COUNTIF(B2:B8, "Omar Haddad")      → 2

Before cleaning, that count was invisibly split between " Omar Haddad" and "omar haddad " and the total said 1 (or worse, if the AI’s tests were looking for capitalization variants). Multiply that by every column in every sheet you’ll ever touch, and you see why cleaning is worth an entire chapter.

The deduping honesty deserves its own moment

With clean names, finding duplicates becomes the fun kind of problem. Two AI-assistable moves:

Move 1 — flag duplicates next to the data:

=IF(COUNTIF($B$2:B2, B2)>1, "dupe", "first")

Read aloud (with help if needed, Chapter 2): “As I fill this down, it counts how many times this name appears in the rows up to and including this one; more than once means ‘dupe’.” Fill down and the second Omar Haddad reads dupe. Nothing is deleted; you see everything and decide.

Move 2 — keep only firsts in one pass, when you’re ready: Data → Remove duplicates (Sheets / Excel) or Data → More Filters → Standard Filter → no duplications (LibreOffice), applied to column B. Run it once, check the count dropped from 7 rows to 6, then delete column B if you no longer need the helper.

The sheets rule of thumb. Don’t destructively clean in columns people care about until you’ve checked the cleaned version beside the original. Duplicate-to-column-B (or duplicate the sheet — see our friend from Chapter 2), sanity-check, then replace. Slow is smooth.

Merging first and last names into one column

The other classic mess is its mirror image: a spreadsheet where names are bisected into First Name / Last Name columns, and now you wish they were one. The glue is:

=A2 & " " & B2

The & is the “glue these two pieces of text together” symbol, and " " in the middle is the space between them. So A2 = Maya, B2 = Chen → Maya Chen. Your AI will sometimes write this as =CONCATENATE(A2," ",B2) or =TEXTJOIN(" ",TRUE,A2:B2) — all the same job, different dialects.

When a mess is bigger than three formulas

Three formulas handle 90% of column mess. The other 10% is where you say the true sentence of this whole book out loud:

“Here’s what column A looks like, here’s what I want it to look like, here are three examples of rows — write me the formula or walk me through the tool for the rest.”

Examples include: names stuck in a single column as "Tanaka, June"; product codes like CANDLE-3PK-2026 where you only want the middle; a date column that says Jan 5, 2026 as words, not a date. Messes like these have tools — “Split text to columns,” Flash Fill (Excel), TEXTBEFORE/TEXTAFTER (newer Excel/Sheets), REGEXEXTRACT (Sheets) — and knowing the name of the kind of tool matters less than describing the want and letting the AI pick. That’s Chapter 1’s job, and Chapter 8 is about what comes after.
