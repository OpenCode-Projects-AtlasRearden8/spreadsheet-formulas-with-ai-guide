Chapter 6 · Pivot Tables Without the Panic

Pivot tables have a reputation as Expert Territory. They aren’t. A pivot table is a machine that answers “group by X, total Y” — the same question you already solved with SUMIF in Chapter 4, just for every group at once, and without writing any formulas.

The job, in one breath

June wants: “How much revenue did each category bring in?” Here is the answer, as a pivot table:

Category Sum of Line Total
Candles 216.00
Gift Sets 294.00
Refills 74.00

And when the same question gets asked with a different lens — “what about by region?” — the click-path is identical:

Region Sum of Line Total
Midwest 193.00
Northeast 79.00
South 114.00
West 198.00

Two details worth pausing on, both reassuring:

  • Every table here sums to 584.00 — the grand total from Chapter 1. When different views of the data agree like that, you’ve almost certainly set everything up right.
  • Nothing about making them required you to type a formula. The pivot table is the viewing tool; SUMIF is the building tool. Both live in your toolbox now.

The four boxes, in plain English

Google Sheets: select your data (A1:H13), then Insert → Pivot table → New sheet — and you meet four little boxes:

Box Meaning
Rows “Group by…” — one row per distinct value (each Category, each Customer, …)
Columns “…and split across…” — an optional second lens; leave it empty today
Values “…and show me…” — the thing that gets summed, counted, or averaged
Filters “…but only for rows where…” — optional spotlight

Four boxes. Plain English names. For June’s category revenue, fill in exactly two of them:

  1. Rows: Category
  2. Values: Line Total, summarized as SUM

That’s the entire table. (Excel calls the boxes by the same names in the “PivotTable Fields” pane; LibreOffice Calc calls the feature DataPilot and hides the same four fields behind friendlier labels once you know to find Insert → Pivot Table.)

The written guide you’re reading now is the slow, careful version — the click-by-click version is genuinely better taught beside your AI, and the prompt for that is one Chapter 1 shape you already know:

I'm about to make my first pivot table in Google Sheets. My data is in
A1:H13; Category is column G, Line Total is column I (=E*F, filled all
the way down). I want: Rows = Category, Values = Sum of Line Total.
Confirm that's the right setup, walk me through the click path one
step at a time, and warn me about any common beginner pitfalls before
I click anything.

Two honest answers you’re likely to get, so you’re not surprised:

  1. Your Line Total column must be filled all the way down. A blank at the bottom silently shortens the sums; a pivot “finding” your last row is a classic reason for a total that looks 80% right.
  2. Start your selection at the header row (A1), not the data rows, so the pivot finds column labels and shows friendly names instead of “Column G.”

Why this doesn’t require panic

Pivot tables sound like they rewrite your data. They really, truly don’t. A few facts, so you can relax:

  • The output is a summary that lives on its own sheet (or a corner of this one). Your orders data is untouched.
  • Deleting costs nothing (right-click → Delete → whole sheet, or Ctrl+Z).
  • Updating costs nothing except a refresh when the data changes (Sheets revises pivots on reload; Excel has an obvious “Refresh” button).
  • They coexist peacefully with formulas — SUMIF calculations on one sheet, a pivot on another, never arguing.

This satisfies our rule from Chapter 0: keep a way to check by hand. Your hand-check here is beautiful: the pivot’s grand total should equal =SUM(I2:I13) from your orders sheet — 584.00 each. You get to watch two independent tools agree, which is worth more than any tutorial.

When to pivot, when to SUMIF

You want… Best tool
One group’s total (“Gift Sets only”) SUMIF
Every group’s totals, stacked Pivot table
Counts across several cut-lines (“orders → count per region and per category”) Pivot table
A number to feed the next formula (e.g., “Gift Sets share of total”) SUMIF, plainly in a cell

That last row is quiet, practical wisdom. Pivots are great to read but awkward to build on top of. SUMIF is less impressive to look at, but its answer sits in a nice ordinary cell you can reference freely. Chapter 4 got you both, which is exactly why it came first.
