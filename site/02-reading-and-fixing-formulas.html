Chapter 2 · Reading and Fixing a Formula

First: you will never be fluent in formulas from reading this once. Nobody is. The trick isn’t memorization — it’s reading a formula the way you read a to-do list, and having a routine for when something comes back wrong.

Reading a formula out loud

Take June’s revenue formula from Chapter 1:

=SUM(I2:I13)

Read it like a sentence: “Sum — add up — the range from cell I2 to cell I13.” That’s all it says. It’s not magic; it’s a to-do list with cell addresses.

Now =E2*F2: “Times the qty in E2 by the price in F2.” The pattern is always:

= FUNCTION( arguments )
  • The = means “this cell does a job.”
  • The function name (SUM, VLOOKUP, SUMIF…) is the verb.
  • The arguments, separated by commas, are its details.
  • A range like I2:I13 means “cells I2 through I13, inclusive.”

When an AI hands you a formula, recite it aloud, and if it’s longer than a line, ask the AI directly:

Please translate this formula into plain English, one step per line: =SUMIF(G2:G13,"Gift Sets",I2:I13)

You’ll get something like:

  1. Look down the Category column (G2:G13).
  2. Where the category is exactly “Gift Sets”…
  3. …add up the matching row’s line total from I2:I13.

Glance at June’s data — Gift Sets are orders 1001, 1004, 1006, 1010 with line totals 84.00 + 42.00 + 42.00 + 126.00 — and you’ll see the answer should be 294.00. Which is what that formula produces. You just verified an AI-written formula by hand. That’s the whole chapter in one move, and you’re more capable than you thought.

Filling down, the honest way

The formula in I2 says =E2*F2. Copy it to I3 and spreadsheets are smart enough that it becomes =E3*F3 on its own — the references “shift” as you copy. This is called a relative reference, and it’s usually what you want. When you don’t want shifting (see Chapter 3’s catalog range), the AI will use dollar signs — catalog!$A$2:$B$5 — which means “this exact range, don’t shift it.” If that sounds abstract, no need to master it today; just know $ pins a reference in place.

Also worth knowing: SUM(A2:A13) that includes a blank cell is fine — blanks count as zero. An error usually means a word got in, not a blank.

When it comes back wrong: the error glossary

Errors look scary and shouty. Read them as polite complaints, each with a specific meaning:

You see Plain meaning Usual first suspect
#NAME? Spreadsheet doesn’t recognize a word you typed Misspelled function name, or quotes missing around text
#N/A “I looked for that and found nothing” Lookup value doesn’t exist in the table (Chapter 3’s topic)
#REF! Formula points at a cell that isn’t there anymore You deleted rows/columns the formula used
#VALUE! You asked math of a word, or vice versa Number stored as text (common with dates — Chapter 5)
#DIV/0! Divide by zero A total in the denominator is 0 or blank
#SPILL! (Excel) The formula wants to output multiple cells but they’re not empty Something stuck in the way
Err:5xx / Err:52x (LibreOffice) Same complaints, different accents Same triage

The fixing routine (a conversation, not a spell)

When a formula gives you an error or a number you don’t believe, run this 5-step loop. You run it with the AI, and nothing more is required of you than honesty:

  1. State the intent. “This was supposed to total revenue by category. I expected 294.00 for Gift Sets.”
  2. State the reality. “I got 0.” (Or #N/A, or 2,940.00 — say the exact thing you saw.)
  3. Give the map. Re-paste your sheet map. AI can’t fix formulas it can’t see.
  4. Ask for diagnosis, then a candidate. “What would cause that? Give me a corrected formula to try.”
  5. Ask for a hand check. “How could I verify the answer with just one manual addition?”

Real example from the writing of this book:

You: My formula =SUMIF(G2:G13,"Gift Sets",I2:I13) returns 0. I calculated by hand that Gift Sets should be 84.00 + 42.00 + 42.00 + 126.00 = 294.00. What’s going on?

AI: If your line totals are in column I and your categories in G, that formula should be right — which means we’re likely missing the categories matching “Gift Sets” exactly. Check whether G2:G13 contains things like Gift Sets (trailing space), gift sets (lowercase), or Gift Set (singular). Tell me what the exact text in G2 is.

Notice: the AI didn’t just spit a new formula and pray. It made a hypothesis and asked you for evidence. That’s a good sign your AI is being a good collaborator — and exactly how Chapter 8’s follow-up loop begins.

Why manual checks matter — the self-trust loop

Every chapter in this book includes a number you can verify by hand (like 294.00 above). When a spreadsheet and your own quick check agree, that builds trust faster than any tutorial can. When they don’t agree, you found a real problem while it’s still tiny. Either way you win. The AI is a fast, patient, occasionally-wrong colleague — your hand check is the safety net.

One formula at a time, in a sheet you can undo

Final practical advice, especially if you’re uneasy in spreadsheets:

  • Open the copy of the file — never the original — and duplicate your sheet (right-click the tab, “Duplicate”) before big changes. Now mistakes cost nothing.
  • Make one formula at a time, sanity-check it, then continue.
  • If a formula builds on a formula, give the AI the whole stack (“I have =E2*F2 in I2, =SUM(I2:I13) in I15, and a =SUMIF in I17”) when you ask.

That’s habits, not heroics. On to Chapter 3 and the famous one: VLOOKUP.
