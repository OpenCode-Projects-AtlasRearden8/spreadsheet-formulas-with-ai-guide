Chapter 3 · VLOOKUP, Without the Fear

VLOOKUP has a reputation. It’s the formula with its own fan club and its own anxiety support group. Here’s the secret: it’s just “look up one thing in a table, and bring back something next to it.” You do this every time you search your contacts for a name and read off the phone number.

The job, in plain English

June’s order sheet (examples/orders.csv) lists products by name, and her price list lives in a second sheet (examples/catalog.csv):

A B C
1 Product UnitPrice Category
2 Candle Trio Gift Box 42.00 Gift Sets
3 Soy Wax Refill Kit 18.50 Refills
4 Beeswax Pillars 24.00 Candles
5 Tea Light Sampler 12.00 Candles

The classic everyday need: “In my order sheet, for each product, look up its current catalog price next to it, so I can check the order sheet’s prices against the master list.”

That’s VLOOKUP’s entire job: V = vertical (it searches down a column), lookup (it finds your value, then brings back a neighbor).

Ask the AI with your two-sheet map

I have two sheets.

Orders sheet: headers in row 1, data in rows 2–13.
  A = OrderID, B = Date, C = Customer, D = Product, E = Qty,
  F = UnitPrice, G = Category, H = Region

Catalog sheet: headers in row 1, data in rows 2–5.
  A = Product, B = UnitPrice

In Orders, column J, look up each product's UnitPrice from the
Catalog sheet (match on Product name). I'm using Google Sheets.
Give me the formula for J2 and tell me if I need to fill it down.

What comes back:

J2:  =VLOOKUP(D2, Catalog!$A$2:$B$5, 2, FALSE)

Fill it down J3…J13 and every order row now shows its catalog price.

Reading it aloud — and the two scary numbers

=VLOOKUP(D2, Catalog!$A$2:$B$5, 2, FALSE):

  1. Take the product name in D2.
  2. Search column A of the Catalog sheet, rows 2–5 (the $ pins the range so it doesn’t shift when you fill down — see Chapter 2).
  3. When you find it, come back with the value from the 2nd column of that range (A is 1st, B is 2nd — so this is the UnitPrice).
  4. FALSE means exact match only.

Two things to memorize — and this is the whole of VLOOKUP’s hard part:

  • The 2 is a column number within the range you gave it, not spreadsheet column B. If your range were A:C, the 2 would still be “the second column of the range,” which would be column B.
  • The FALSE insists on an exact match. Leave it off (or say TRUE) and the function happily hands you the nearest wrong answer. Your AI writing FALSE for you is a small kindness.

Verify one by hand

Order 1008 is Omar Haddad, Beeswax Pillars. J column lookup for that row should read 24.00 — exactly the Beeswax price from the catalog. Check one cell against the price list with your eyes and you now trust the other eleven.

When VLOOKUP gives you #N/A

#N/A after a lookup isn’t a scolding. It’s the most polite error in spreadsheet land: “I looked; I found nothing.” (See the glossary in Chapter 2.) In practice it almost always means one of:

Cause Example What to do
Spelling/spacing mismatch Order says Beeswax Pillars (trailing space), catalog says Beeswax Pillars Clean the column — Chapter 7
It’s genuinely absent Order sheet contains “Wick Trimmer Set” but catalog doesn’t Add it to the catalog, or accept and flag the row
Wrong first column Catalog’s lookup column is actually B Point the formula at the true leftmost column

And this is where AI shines — a typical honest exchange goes:

You: My J column gets #N/A in some rows with this formula: =VLOOKUP(D2, Catalog!$A$2:$B$5, 2, FALSE). D13 is Tea Light Sampler and the catalog definitely has it. Why only some rows?

AI: If it’s only some rows, the value itself exists — those lookup cells probably have invisible differences: extra spaces, or the text sitting in the catalog with different capitalization. In a blank cell, try =D2=Catalog!A2 style checks for two of the failing pairs… actually, simpler: run =LEN(D13) on the failing cell and tell me the number. If it’s longer than Tea Light Sampler, we’ll count the suspects.

You: LEN says 19.

AI: Tea Light Sampler is 17 characters. Two extra ones — almost certainly leading or trailing spaces. Let’s clean the column; want a formula for K2 like =TRIM(D2) to fill down, or are you up for fixing it in place?

(That cleanup is exactly Chapter 7.)

The #N/A with eyes. If you’d rather see friendly text than #N/A, ask the AI: “wrap it so an unmatched lookup shows ‘not in catalog’ instead of #N/A.” You’ll get an IFERROR wrapper: =IFERROR(VLOOKUP(D2, Catalog!$A$2:$B$5, 2, FALSE), "not in catalog"). Same logic, kinder face.

Sidebar: XLOOKUP, the younger sibling

If you’re on recent Excel or Google Sheets, you may also meet XLOOKUP, which does the same job with clearer arguments (lookup range, return range, no “2nd column” counting). The AI can give you either one — and VLOOKUP remains worth learning because half the spreadsheets on Earth use it. Try asking the AI for both versions of this formula and see them side by side; it’s a genuinely instructive five minutes.
