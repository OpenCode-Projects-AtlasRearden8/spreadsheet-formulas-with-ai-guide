Chapter 4 · SUMIF and COUNTIF

SUM adds everything. SUMIF adds the ones you want. COUNTIF counts the ones you want. That’s the entire difference, and it’s why these two functions will quietly become the ones you reach for most.

The everyday question

Looking at June’s January orders again: not just “how much revenue?” (chapter 1 answered that, 584.00) but things like:

  • How much came from Gift Sets? (a single line — the question every small business asks when restocking)
  • How many orders were candles?
  • How much revenue from the Midwest?

Notice each question has the same skeleton: add/count something, but only where some condition holds. SUMIF and COUNTIF are built for exactly that shape.

Asking the AI, with the sheet map

My sheet has headers in row 1 and data in rows 2–13.
A = OrderID, B = Date, C = Customer, D = Product, E = Qty,
F = UnitPrice, G = Category, H = Region
I = Line Total (=E*F, filled down)

Give me three formulas:
1. Total line-total revenue where Category is "Gift Sets"
2. Count of orders where Category is "Candles"
3. Total line-total revenue where Region is "Midwest"
Put each in a separate empty cell in row 16, like 16A/16B/16C labels
described to me. I'm using Google Sheets.

What comes back:

=SUMIF(G2:G13, "Gift Sets", I2:I13)      → the Gift Sets revenue
=COUNTIF(G2:G13, "Candles")              → how many candle orders
=SUMIF(H2:H13, "Midwest", I2:I13)        → the Midwest revenue

And the expected answers (which our project’s test suite also computes independently):

Formula Result
SUMIF Gift Sets 294.00 (orders 84.00 + 42.00 + 42.00 + 126.00)
COUNTIF Candles 5
SUMIF Midwest 193.00

Reading one aloud

=SUMIF(G2:G13, "Gift Sets", I2:I13), translated:

  1. Walk down G2:G13 (the Category column)…
  2. …stopping where the cell says exactly Gift Sets…
  3. …and when you find one, add that row’s value from I2:I13.

The shape is always the same:

=SUMIF( <range to test>, <test>, <range to add> )
=COUNTIF( <range to test>, <test> )

COUNTIF has no third argument — counting doesn’t need an “add” range.

The one pitfall worth knowing: exactly what “matches”

SUMIF/COUNTIF match on the exact text, ignoring capitalization but not ignoring extra spaces. So Gift Sets (trailing space) is not Gift Sets, and gift sets is Gift Sets. Chapter 2 met this gremlin; Chapter 7 evicts it for good.

If a SUMIF returns 0 when you “know” it shouldn’t, tell the AI exactly that (Chapter 2’s fixing routine), and it’ll likely ask to see the exact text in one of the tested cells. Often it’s a space in hiding.

Two conditions at once: SUMIFS / COUNTIFS

“Yes, but Gift Sets and the West region” — that’s SUMIFS (with an S). The argument order flips (the sum range comes first):

=SUMIFS(I2:I13, G2:G13, "Gift Sets", H2:H13, "West")

Read aloud: “From the line totals, add rows where Category is Gift Sets and Region is West.” That’s order 1010, worth 126.00. The S versions take condition pairs like a list of rules: range/what, range2/what2, and so on.

Sanity-check it: our test suite computes this same SUMIFS and confirms 126.00 exactly. When your sheet says 126.00 too, you’ve understood it.

One more useful shape: cells whose totals you want for every value

Later you’ll want not just Gift Sets but every category at once, each with its total next to it. Two options, both worth knowing:

  1. One SUMIF per label. In a small block, type each category (Candles, Gift Sets, Refills), and next to each one the AI’s formula pointing at its row. Yes, three explicit rows, three formulas (or a single filled-down one with a $ range and a reference to the label cell).
  2. A pivot table. Which is literally purpose-built for exactly this shape of question, and is Chapter 6.

You’ll meet the category revenue table in full (Candles 216.00, Gift Sets 294.00, Refills 74.00) in Chapter 6.
