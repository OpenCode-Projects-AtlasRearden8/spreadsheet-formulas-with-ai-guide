Chapter 5 · Dates Without Dread

If formulas have a bogeyman, it’s dates. “1/5/2026” looks like text, but your spreadsheet secretly treats it as a number — and once you see that, dates stop being scary and start being useful.

The big secret: dates are numbers wearing costumes

In Excel, Google Sheets, and LibreOffice Calc, a date is just a number — how many days since some long-ago start point — displayed as a date. Try it if you like: if June’s order date cell B2 is a real date, then

=B2 + 20

gives back 2026-01-25. Add 20 days and you’re twenty days later. That secret explains almost everything about dates:

  • Arithmetic “just works” on well-formed date cells (B3-B2 is days between them).
  • Sorting by date is really sorting by number.
  • “What’s wrong with my date formula” is usually “this cell is secretly text, not a number.”

Your first useful date formulas

=B2 + 30                    → 30 days after Jan 5 → 2026-02-04
=B13 - B2                   → Jan 25 minus Jan 5    → 20 (days)
=DAYS(B13, B2)              → the same 20, spelled out
=EOMONTH(B2, 0)             → the last day of B2's month → 2026-01-31
=TEXT(B2, "mmmm yyyy")     → the wordy label → "January 2026"
=YEAR(B2), =MONTH(B2), =WEEKDAY(B2)

Every one of these is verifiable by hand with 12 rows of January data — that’s the point of the small dataset all book long. Ask your AI first (“with my sheet map: B = Date…”), then confirm the result yourself.

The real-life trap: 1/5/2026 typed into the wrong place

Here’s the classic, and it hits nurses’ shift logs as hard as candle orders. Someone types 1/5/2026 in a spreadsheet set to European international formatting, or a cell was pre-formatted as Text, or the value came from a cut-and-paste from a website. Same-looking cell, four possible realities:

  • A real date, meaning January 5, 2026
  • A real date, meaning May 1, 2026 (column formatted differently)
  • The words “1/5/2026” sitting there as text (left-aligned!)
  • Empty, with the source typing evicted by auto-correct

Three warnings that catch most cases:

  1. Alignment is the tell. Real numbers (and real dates) line up right; text lines up left in a fresh General-formatted column.
  2. =B2+1 either works (real date), or produces an error or a strange number (text).
  3. =ISNUMBER(B2) returns TRUE for a true date, FALSE for the impostor. This humble check may be the most useful formula in this book you’ve never needed before.

If you find impostor text-dates, don’t fix 12 cells by re-typing; ask the AI (“make these text cells real dates”), which will hand you a DATEVALUE formula or a one-click Data → Split text/column settings approach — whatever is right for your program.

Fictional footnote: all dates used in this book are from a fictional January 2026 dataset in examples/orders.csv. No real orders, no real people, no personal data.

SUMIFS with a date window: the useful one

The classic real-life question: “how much between January 11 and January 20?” A date range is just two conditions on the same Date column. With your sheet map and the fix-it-routine from Chapter 2:

=SUMIFS(I2:I13, B2:B13, ">="&DATE(2026,1,11), B2:B13, "<="&DATE(2026,1,20))

Translation: add line totals of the rows whose date is on or after January 11, 2026 and on or before January 20, 2026. That’s orders 1004–1009 — six of them — and the answer is 253.00 (a SUMIFS our test suite computes independently, too). The ">="&DATE(...) sandwich wall of symbols looks worse than it is: the first piece, ">="&DATE(...), is AI-written for you, and you only need to know that it means “on or after.”

If this is your first time seeing DATE(2026,1,11): it builds a date from your own year, month, day — safer than typing 1/11/2026 into a formula, whose meaning can change based on your computer’s country settings (the very trap from earlier).

A friendly closing word about this chapter

Dates have a reputation for being technical, and this chapter is long — but here is the entire take-home list:

  • a date is a number in disguise,
  • +1 steps a day, subtracting gives a gap in days,
  • ISNUMBER sniffs impostors,
  • SUMIFS with two date conditions is how you say “between two dates.”

Everything else you look up when you need it, ideally in the chat window, in plain English — that’s the method of this whole book.
